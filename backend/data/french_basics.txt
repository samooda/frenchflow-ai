## Être vs Avoir in Passé Composé

Most French verbs form the passé composé with avoir as the auxiliary verb.
Example: J'ai mangé (I ate), Tu as parlé (You spoke), Il a fini (He finished).

A fixed group of verbs — mostly verbs of motion or change of state — use être instead.
Common être verbs include: aller (to go), venir (to come), partir (to leave), arriver (to arrive), naître (to be born), mourir (to die), rester (to stay), tomber (to fall), and all reflexive verbs.
Example: Je suis allé(e) (I went), Elle est partie (She left), Nous nous sommes levés (We got up).

When using être, the past participle must agree in gender and number with the subject.
Example: Il est arrivé. / Elle est arrivée. / Ils sont arrivés. / Elles sont arrivées.


## Adjective Agreement

French adjectives must agree in gender (masculine/feminine) and number (singular/plural) with the noun they modify.
Most adjectives add -e for feminine: petit → petite, grand → grande.
Most adjectives add -s for masculine plural and -es for feminine plural: petits, petites.

Some adjectives are irregular. Common patterns:
- Adjectives ending in -eux become -euse in the feminine: heureux → heureuse (happy).
- Adjectives ending in -er become -ère in the feminine: dernier → dernière (last).
- Adjectives ending in -al become -aux in the masculine plural: national → nationaux.

Most adjectives follow the noun in French: une voiture rouge (a red car).
A small group of common adjectives precede the noun, remembered with the acronym BAGS: Beauty (beau, joli), Age (jeune, vieux, nouveau), Goodness (bon, mauvais), Size (grand, petit, long).


## Gendered Nouns

Every French noun has a grammatical gender — masculine or feminine. There is no neutral gender.
The gender must be memorized with each noun, but some endings are reliable indicators.

Common masculine endings: -age (le voyage), -ment (le gouvernement), -eur (le serveur), -isme (le tourisme).
Common feminine endings: -tion (la nation), -sion (la décision), -ette (la baguette), -ité (la liberté), -ure (la voiture).

Nouns referring to people often have both forms: un étudiant / une étudiante, un acteur / une actrice.
Some nouns use the same form for both genders, distinguished only by the article: un/une élève (student).


## Definite and Indefinite Articles

Definite articles refer to a specific or known noun. They agree with the gender and number of the noun.
le = masculine singular (le livre — the book)
la = feminine singular (la table — the table)
l' = before a vowel or silent h (l'ami, l'heure)
les = all plurals (les livres, les tables)

Indefinite articles refer to an unspecified noun.
un = masculine singular (un chat — a cat)
une = feminine singular (une maison — a house)
des = plural (des chats — some cats / cats)

After a negation, indefinite and partitive articles change to de (or d' before a vowel).
Example: J'ai un chien. → Je n'ai pas de chien. (I don't have a dog.)


## Partitive Articles

Partitive articles express an unspecified quantity of something uncountable — roughly equivalent to "some" in English.
du = masculine singular (du pain — some bread)
de la = feminine singular (de la viande — some meat)
de l' = before a vowel or silent h (de l'eau — some water)
des = plural (des fruits — some fruit)

They are commonly used with food, drink, and abstract concepts.
Example: Je bois du café. (I drink coffee.) / Elle a de la patience. (She has patience.)

After negation, all partitive articles become de (or d').
Example: Je mange du pain. → Je ne mange pas de pain.


## Negation

Standard French negation uses two parts: ne … pas, placed around the conjugated verb.
Example: Je parle. → Je ne parle pas. (I don't speak.)

In spoken French, ne is frequently dropped, leaving just pas: Je parle pas. This is informal.

Other common negation pairs:
- ne … jamais (never): Il ne fume jamais.
- ne … rien (nothing): Je ne vois rien.
- ne … plus (no longer / not anymore): Elle ne travaille plus ici.
- ne … personne (nobody): Je ne connais personne.

In compound tenses like passé composé, ne and pas wrap the auxiliary verb.
Example: Je n'ai pas mangé. (I didn't eat.) / Il n'est pas venu. (He didn't come.)


## Question Formation

There are three main ways to ask a yes/no question in French.

1. Rising intonation (spoken French, informal): Tu parles français? (You speak French?)
2. Est-ce que + statement: Est-ce que tu parles français?
3. Inversion — place the verb before the subject and add a hyphen: Parles-tu français?
   When the verb ends in a vowel and the subject is il/elle/on, add -t- for pronunciation: Parle-t-il?

For information questions, add a question word before est-ce que or use inversion.
Common question words: Qui (who), Que/Quoi (what), Où (where), Quand (when), Comment (how), Pourquoi (why), Combien (how much/many).
Example: Où est-ce que tu habites? / Où habites-tu? (Where do you live?)


## Passé Composé: Regular Verb Conjugation

The passé composé is the most common past tense in spoken French. It expresses completed actions.
It is formed with: subject + auxiliary verb (avoir or être, conjugated in present) + past participle.

Regular past participles are formed by dropping the infinitive ending and adding a new suffix:
- -er verbs add -é: parler → parlé
- -ir and -re verbs drop their ending and add -i or -u respectively: finir → fini, vendre → vendu

Full example with avoir (parler):
j'ai parlé, tu as parlé, il/elle a parlé, nous avons parlé, vous avez parlé, ils/elles ont parlé.

Many common verbs have irregular past participles that must be memorized.
Examples: être → été, avoir → eu, faire → fait, prendre → pris, voir → vu, boire → bu, pouvoir → pu.


## Pronunciation: Silent Letters and Liaison

French has many silent final consonants. The last consonant of a word is usually not pronounced.
Examples: vous (the s is silent), grand (the d is silent), beaucoup (the p is silent).

However, liaison occurs when a normally silent final consonant is pronounced because the next word begins with a vowel or silent h. This links the two words in speech.
Examples: vous avez → "vou-zavez", les enfants → "lay-zenfants", un ami → "u-nami".

Liaison is mandatory in some contexts (after articles, pronouns, short adjectives before nouns) and forbidden in others (after et, before an aspirate h like les haricots).

The letter e at the end of a word is usually silent, but it affects the pronunciation of the preceding consonant.
Example: grand (d silent) vs grande (d is pronounced because of the final e).


## Common Spelling Patterns: Accents

French uses five types of diacritical marks that change pronunciation or distinguish meaning.

1. accent aigu (é): produces the "ay" sound. été (summer), café.
2. accent grave (è, à, ù): è produces an open "eh" sound (père — father). à and ù distinguish words: a (has) vs à (at/to), ou (or) vs où (where).
3. accent circonflexe (â, ê, î, ô, û): often marks a historical dropped s, slightly changes vowel quality. forêt (forest), hôtel, fête (party).
4. tréma (ë, ï, ü): indicates the vowel is pronounced separately from the previous one. Noël (Christmas), naïf (naive).
5. cédille (ç): makes c produce an "s" sound before a, o, u. façade, garçon (boy), français.
